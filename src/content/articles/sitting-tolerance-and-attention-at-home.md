You sit your child down with a worksheet. Within 90 seconds the pencil is on the floor, the chair has been pushed back, and the two of you are having the same argument you had yesterday. By the end of the week you are dreading the moment more than your child is.

The instinct is to push for longer. Sit properly. Five more minutes. Finish this page. It almost never works, because the problem is not willingness. Sitting and attending are physical skills, built the way any physical skill is built, from a length your child can already manage.

So the plan below does the opposite of pushing. It starts shorter than you think is useful, and it stops while things are still going well.

## Start at your child's real limit, not the one you wish for

Watch for 3 days without changing anything. How long does your child stay at a table on something an adult chose, before getting up or melting down? Two minutes counts. Ninety seconds counts.

That number is your starting point. If it is 2 minutes, you practise 2 minutes, and you end at 2 minutes even when the session is going beautifully. Ending early is the whole trick, because it is the ending that your child remembers.

Hold that length for about a week, then add 30 seconds. Not 5 minutes. Thirty seconds, held for another week. Progress this slow feels like nothing on a Tuesday and looks like a lot after 8 weeks, so write the number down.

## Set up the chair, the table and the room

A child who is fighting the furniture cannot attend to the work. Check 3 things. Feet flat on the floor or on a firm box, not swinging. Knees and hips at roughly right angles. The table at about elbow height when the shoulders are relaxed.

Most dining chairs in a Dhaka flat are built for adults, so a footrest is often the single fastest change you can make. A stack of old books, tied with a cloth, does the job.

Then take the room down a notch. Television off, not muted. Phone face down and out of reach. Toys on the table cleared away, other than the one thing you are working on. If your flat is small and the grandmother is watching her serial in the same room, borrow a corner of the bedroom for 5 minutes rather than fighting for silence you cannot get.

Try to use the same chair, the same table and the same time of day. Routine carries a child through moments where willpower does not.

## Show the time, then use first and then

Children have almost no sense of how long 3 minutes is, so they treat every request as open ended, and open ended is frightening. A visual timer fixes this: a sand timer, or a clock app where a coloured wedge shrinks as time runs out. Your child needs to see the end approaching, not be told about it.

Pair it with first and then language, which is short, calm and does not invite a debate. "First 3 puzzle pieces, then the ball." "First we write 2 lines, then the timer finishes and we are done." Say it once, point at the timer, then stop talking. Repeating yourself turns the instruction into a negotiation.

Say what comes after, and make it something your child actually wants. Not a reward chart that pays out on Friday, but the thing that happens in the next 30 seconds: a favourite toy, a song, a run down the corridor.

## Finish before it falls apart, and praise the sitting

Here is the rule that matters most. End the session while your child is still doing well, ideally 10 seconds before the wheels come off.

You will learn the warning signs: the foot starts tapping, the eyes go to the door, the voice changes. Do not try to squeeze one more item out. Close the book, say "you finished, well done for staying in your chair," and let your child go.

Notice the wording. Praise the sitting, not the output. "You stayed at the table for the whole timer" is precise and repeatable. "Good boy" is warm but tells your child nothing about what to do again tomorrow. If the handwriting was untidy, let it be untidy this week. You are training the chair, not the letters.

Movement is part of the plan, not a failure of it. Many children attend better after 2 minutes of heavy work: carrying a bag of rice to the kitchen, pushing against a wall, 10 jumps, a squeeze from you. Build the movement break into the routine before the fidgeting starts, so it is part of the session and not a reward for falling apart.

Expect bad days. A late night, a hot afternoon, a long ride back over Rampura bridge in traffic, and the 4 minutes your child managed yesterday will not be there. Drop the length for that day, keep the routine, and come back to the number tomorrow.

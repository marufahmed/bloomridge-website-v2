The question we hear most often is a version of the same one. Should we wait? He is only 3. An uncle did not speak until he was 4 and he is an engineer now.

Waiting is a completely reasonable instinct. Nobody wants to turn an ordinary slow patch into a problem, and the advice from family is usually kind and always confident. Meanwhile you are the one noticing that your child does not turn when called, or plays alone at every birthday party, or has 10 words when the cousin of the same age has 200.

You do not have to decide today whether something is wrong. You only have to decide whether your child could use some practice, and that is a much smaller question.

## What the years from 2 to 6 are building

This is not a door that slams shut. Children keep learning at every age, and no parent should be frightened into anything. What is true is that the years from 2 to 6 are when foundations get laid cheaply, because a young child practises all day without noticing.

Three foundations matter most. Communication, which starts long before words with pointing, looking and taking turns in a back-and-forth. Regulation, which is the ability to stay in a workable state when something is loud or disappointing. And connecting with others, which is what turns a child playing beside another child into a child playing with them.

These stack on top of each other. Joint attention comes before words, words before sentences, sitting and listening before reading. A gap early on is not a ceiling, but it does mean the next skill waits, and after 2 or 3 years of waiting the distance between your child and the classroom has grown on its own.

## You do not need a diagnosis to start

We do not diagnose, and we do not need a diagnosis to work. Support is built from what a child can and cannot do right now. A 3-year-old with 10 words needs vocabulary, turn-taking and reasons to communicate, whatever the eventual explanation turns out to be.

Many parents hesitate because of the label. The worry is usually specific: that a word will follow the child through school, that relatives will treat them differently, that a decision made at 3 will close something at 13. That worry deserves a straight answer rather than reassurance.

An assessment here is not a diagnosis and does not produce one. What we write is a description of skills, and it stays with you. Nothing goes to a school unless you send it. Where a specialist opinion would genuinely help, a paediatrician or developmental specialist is the right person to see, and we will say so rather than keep you here. When a diagnosis does come, it works like a key to services rather than a description of what your child can become.

## What "wait and see" usually costs

Usually it costs a year, and rarely the cheapest year to lose. Waiting from 3 to 4 means waiting through the last year before school, exactly when sitting, listening and following instructions are supposed to be built.

Being honest about the other side: some children do catch up without any help. The difficulty is that nobody can tell in advance which child that will be, so it is worth comparing the two mistakes. If you start support for a child who would have been fine anyway, your child gets extra practice and good play, and you get better at reading them. If you wait with a child who needed help, you lose a year, and frustration usually hardens into behaviour that then takes its own time to undo.

## What early intervention looks like in an ordinary week

The Early Intervention Program is usually for children aged 2 to 6, and it works on communication, sensory processing and connecting with others.

In practice, every plan includes school sessions 4 days a week for 2 hours in a small group, where classroom routines are practised: arriving, sitting, listening, waiting, following a 2-step instruction. There is one 2-hour psychologist-led group session a week for turn-taking, peer play and emotional awareness. Then there are 2 to 4 one-to-one therapy sessions a week, depending on whether the plan is Foundation, Enhanced or Intensive. Those may be speech and language therapy for early language, clearer speech and asking for what your child needs, occupational therapy for fine motor skills, sensory regulation and daily independence, or special education, which is one-to-one teaching at your child's own pace.

The part that makes it hold together is the daily loop. Therapists hand strategies to teachers, teachers report back what happened in class, and you get a written review each week. At home you get worksheets matched to your child's level, help building a routine, and monthly coaching. If it is a grandmother who brings your child by rickshaw and spends most of the day with them, she is the one who should sit in on that coaching.

## What to expect in the first 3 months

Plans are written in 3-month cycles matched to the school trimesters, with an assessment month in December. Every skill is scored on a 4-step scale: emerging, meaning it appears once or twice with full support; developing, meaning it happens with prompts; consistent, meaning it happens most days with a reminder; and independent, meaning it happens without help and in more than one place.

A realistic first cycle moves a set of skills 1 step, and sometimes 2. That is what genuine progress looks like written down, and it is why we use the scale instead of promises.

The first changes are usually not the ones you were hoping for. Before the words come, you tend to see a child who settles faster on arrival, cries less at drop-off, brings you a cup instead of screaming at the shelf, or tolerates a haircut with only 1 short protest. Those are the foundations moving. Progress is also not a straight line, and a week of fever or a fortnight of broken sleep will show up in the notes.

Nobody here will tell you your child will be talking by a certain date. Anyone who does is guessing.

Admission season arrives before most families feel ready for it. Forms open, cousins compare schools, and someone at a family dinner asks whether your child knows the alphabet yet. If that question makes your stomach drop, you are not the only parent in Banasree who feels it.

Here is the part that is rarely said out loud. Most English-medium school interviews last 10 to 15 minutes, and very little of that is spent on letters. The teacher is watching whether your child walks into an unfamiliar room, sits down, looks up when spoken to, and tries what she is asked to try.

That is good news, because those are ordinary habits you can practise at home, in the 20 minutes before dinner. They also decide whether the first term of school feels manageable or exhausting, long after the interview is forgotten.

## Readiness is a set of small habits, not a syllabus

A child who can recite the alphabet but cannot stay in a chair for 5 minutes will struggle in a classroom of 25. A child who knows 6 letters but can sit, listen, and ask for help will pick up the other 20 letters in a few weeks.

Teachers call the first skill sitting tolerance: staying in one place, on one activity, for long enough to learn something. It sounds simple. For many children it is the hardest part of the school day, and it builds with practice rather than arriving on a birthday.

The same goes for following a 2-step instruction, waiting a turn, and saying "I need help" before frustration takes over. None of it is academic, and all of it is what school asks for from day one.

## A checklist you can actually use

Read each line and answer with one of 3 words: not yet, sometimes, usually. Do not aim for a perfect score. Most 4-year-olds have gaps, and the point is to see where yours are.

### Settling without you

- Says goodbye at the gate or the lift and goes in, with any upset settling within about 5 minutes
- Stays 2 hours with a familiar adult who is not you, such as a grandmother or a class teacher
- Accepts comfort from that adult instead of waiting only for you
- Copes when the plan changes, for example the usual park trip is cancelled

### Sitting, listening and following instructions

- Sits at a table for 5 to 10 minutes on an activity an adult chose, not one your child chose
- Looks up when their name is called across the room
- Follows a 2-step instruction such as "put your shoes in the rack and come to the table"
- Waits about 1 minute for a turn without grabbing
- Stops a favourite activity when given a warning, such as "2 more minutes, then we finish"

### Managing their own body

- Tells an adult about needing the toilet, and manages most of the steps alone
- Washes and dries hands without being walked through each part
- Opens a tiffin box and eats most of it inside 20 minutes
- Puts on shoes and carries their own bag
- Asks for help, with words, a gesture or a picture, rather than freezing or melting down

### Being with other children

- Plays near 2 or 3 other children without becoming distressed
- Takes a turn in a simple game with an adult holding the structure
- Hands a toy back when the turn is over
- Notices when another child is upset, even if the response is clumsy

### Hands, pencils and only then letters

- Holds a crayon or pencil with some control and makes marks on purpose
- Copies a vertical line, a circle and a cross
- Snips along a straight line with child scissors
- Recognises their own name written down
- Counts up to 10 real objects, touching each one, rather than reciting numbers by heart

Notice where letters sit: at the end, and in the smallest group. That order is deliberate. A child who can hold a pencil and sit for 8 minutes is ready to be taught letters. A child who cannot will find letter practice miserable, and so will you.

## What to do if several lines say "not yet"

First, do not start drilling. Doubling the practice on the weakest skill is the fastest way to make your child hate the table.

Pick 2 items, not 10. Work on them for about 6 weeks in ordinary moments: the 2-step instruction while packing the school bag, the waiting while the housing-society lift takes its time, the asking for help while opening a biscuit packet. Short and daily beats long and occasional.

If the gaps are wide, or the same difficulty shows up at home, at the play area and at the grandparents' flat, that is worth a proper look. An assessment is not a verdict. We do not diagnose, and we work with children who have a diagnosis and children who do not.

## How the School Readiness Program rehearses the school day

Our School Readiness Program, usually for children aged 4 to 10, lets classroom life be practised before it is required. Children come 4 days a week for 2 hours of school sessions, with the same arrival routine, the same table and the same expectation of sitting and listening.

Once a week there is a 2-hour group session run by a psychologist, where turn-taking, peer play and naming feelings are the actual lesson rather than something children are left to pick up. Alongside that come 2 to 4 one-to-one therapy sessions a week, depending on the plan: occupational therapy for hands, sensory regulation and daily independence, speech and language therapy for early language, clearer speech and asking for what you need, and special education for one-to-one teaching at your child's pace.

It starts with a strengths-first assessment: we map what your child can already do before we look at what is missing. That becomes a Student Profile covering learning style, communication preferences, sensory needs and motivators, which every therapist, teacher and caregiver works from. Goals are written for 3-month cycles matched to the school trimesters, and you hear how the week went, every week.

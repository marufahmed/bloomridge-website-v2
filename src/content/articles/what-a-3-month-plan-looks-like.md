When a centre hands you a plan for your child, two things usually happen at once. You feel relieved that there is a plan, and you have no idea how to read it. The page has goals on it, and percentages, and words like "prompting" and "generalisation", and you nod politely and take it home.

Then 3 months go by and you still do not know whether it worked.

So this article is about the document itself: where the goals come from, how they are written, who checks them, and what happens when one is not met. We call it an individual education plan, or IEP. It runs for 3 months, and it is meant to be read by you, not filed by us.

## Where the goals come from

Everything starts with the strengths-first assessment. Before we look at what is hard for your child, we map what your child can already do: what holds attention, which instructions get followed, how needs get communicated, what makes your child laugh.

That is not politeness. It is practical. If a 5-year-old will sit for 8 minutes with a puzzle but 40 seconds with a worksheet, we teach through puzzles first and move towards the worksheet later. The strengths are the route.

What comes out of the assessment goes into the Student Profile: learning style, communication preferences, sensory needs, motivators. It is live, so it changes as we learn more, and the occupational therapist, the speech and language therapist, the special educator, the classroom teacher and you are all working from the same one. A plan built on a profile that only one person has read is not really a plan.

## What a goal actually reads like

A goal has to be specific enough that two different people, watching on two different days, would score it the same way.

Imagine a goal that says: follows a 2-step instruction with 1 visual prompt, 4 times out of 5. That is checkable. Anyone can see whether the instruction was 2 steps, whether a picture card was used, and how many times out of 5 it worked.

Compare that to "improves listening skills". Nobody can score that, so nobody can tell you in December whether it happened.

Each goal is tied to 2 things. First, a specific item in our assessment bank, which is the list of skills we test against, so December has something concrete to measure. Second, the curriculum target for that trimester. Our school curriculum states the exact letters, words and numbers taught in each block, so a writing goal in the September to November trimester points at the letters actually being taught then, not letters your child will not meet until March.

## The 4 steps we score against

We do not score a skill as done or not done. Real learning is slower and messier than that, so we use 4 steps.

Emerging means your child has done it once or twice, with a lot of help, and it may disappear tomorrow. Developing means it happens fairly often, but still with prompting from an adult. Consistent means it happens reliably in the place where it was taught, with little or no help. Independent means it happens without prompting, in more than one place: at the centre, at home, and at the grandmother's flat on Friday.

Most of the useful work of a 3-month cycle is the move from developing to consistent. It is also the least visible from outside, which is why the weekly review matters: you see the small movements as they happen instead of waiting for a verdict.

Every week you get a short written review of how the week went, what was tried and what changed. Once a month there is a coaching session for you, with take-home worksheets and activity guides matched to your child's current level, and help building a home routine that survives a real evening with homework, dinner and a tired child.

## Teach from September to November, assess in December

The plan runs on the school's trimester rhythm, deliberately, so your child is not living in two different calendars.

September to November is a teaching block. We teach, we practise, we adjust the method when something is not working, and we do not keep stopping to test. December is the assessment month. We score each goal against its assessment item, write it up, and sit down with you to go through it line by line.

Then the next cycle is written, and the teaching starts again. Your child's plan is being reviewed at about the same time as the school is closing its own term, which means what we tell you in December can go into the conversation you have with the class teacher in January.

## What happens when a goal is not met

Some goals will not be met. That is normal, and it is worth saying plainly because most parents brace for it as though it were a school result.

A goal that is not met is rewritten, not failed. In December we look at why. Sometimes the goal was too big a jump, so we break it into 2 smaller ones. Sometimes the skill is there but only in the therapy room, so the new goal moves it into the classroom and then into your home. Sometimes we were teaching it the wrong way for this particular child, and the change is in our method, not in the target.

And sometimes life got in the way. A long illness, a move, 3 weeks of disrupted sleep. A child who was ill through half of October has not failed anything.

Your child is not scored against other children, and the plan is not a report card. It is a working document with 1 purpose: to make sure that in 3 months we are teaching the right next thing, and that you can tell whether it worked.

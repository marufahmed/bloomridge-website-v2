Most parents who come to us have already had a hard conversation somewhere else. A teacher mentioned something at pick-up. A relative said the wrong thing at a wedding. A report came back full of words nobody explained.

So people arrive at a first assessment expecting another list of what is wrong with their child. That is not how the morning goes here, and it is worth saying before you come.

We start with what your child can already do. Not as a kindness, but because it is the only sensible place to start. The skills a child has are the ones the next step gets built on, and you cannot plan anything useful until you know what they are.

## Strengths first, and what that means in practice

A strengths-first assessment means we map what your child can do before we look at what he cannot. A child who will not sit at a table but will sit for 15 minutes with a bucket of rice and a spoon has a real sitting skill. It just lives somewhere nobody thought to look.

That matters for the plan. If we know your child settles best with something in his hands, the teacher builds that into the classroom instead of fighting it every morning for 3 months. If we know he will work for a turn on the swing, we have a motivator for the whole trimester.

It matters for you too. Parents leave able to describe their child accurately, which is a different experience from leaving with a list of deficits.

## What to bring

Bring any previous reports you have, from a paediatrician, a school, a hospital or another centre, even old ones and ones you disagreed with. We would rather read them than have you summarise from memory.

Bring your child's favourite toy or a familiar snack. A new room with new adults is a lot, and a known object in hand shortens the settling-in time considerably.

Bring a note of the words your child uses, in Bangla or English or both, along with the gestures and sounds he uses to get things. You hear your child all day. We will see him for a couple of hours, in an unfamiliar place, and he may spend the first 20 minutes watching us.

If he naps at a particular time, tell us when you book so we can avoid it. An assessment in the middle of a nap window tells us nothing except that your child is tired.

## What the session actually looks like

It is play, watched carefully. There is no test paper and nothing your child can fail.

Expect around 1 to 2 hours, most of it on the floor with toys, picture cards, bubbles, stacking and posting things, and a short stretch at a small table if your child is old enough for that to matter. One of our team leads the play and follows your child's interests rather than marching through a checklist, because a child who is enjoying himself shows you far more than a child being examined.

While that happens, we are observing across 6 developmental areas: communication, social and emotional development, sensory processing and regulation, fine motor skills, gross motor skills, and self-help and daily living. Those last ones cover the practical business of being a child, such as holding a spoon, managing shoes, washing hands.

There is also a conversation with you, and it is not a formality. What happens at bedtime. What sets off a difficult morning. Whether the lift in your building is a problem, whether the school run by rickshaw is where the trouble starts, what Nanu has noticed that nobody else has. You know things no assessment room will show us.

## The 4-step scale

Every skill we look at is scored on a 4-step scale, and it is deliberately plain so that parents can use it too.

**Emerging** means we saw it once or twice, with a lot of help. **Developing** means it happens sometimes, with prompting. **Consistent** means it happens reliably in a familiar setting with a familiar adult. **Independent** means your child does it on his own, in more than one place, with more than one person.

The distance between consistent and independent is where a lot of real work sits. A child who can ask for water at the centre and not at home has a skill that has not travelled yet, and travelling is the goal.

## What we do not do

We do not diagnose. Bloomridge Springs is not a diagnostic service, and no assessment here will come back with a label attached.

We support children with a diagnosis and children without one, and the plan we write does not depend on having one. If what we see suggests your child should be seen by a paediatrician or a developmental specialist, we will tell you directly and say why. We will not hint at a condition.

## From the assessment to a plan

Everything from the session goes into your child's Student Profile: learning style, communication preferences, sensory needs, motivators, and where each skill sits on the 4-step scale. It is a live document, not a filed report. Every therapist, teacher and caregiver working with your child works from the same profile, and it is updated as your child changes.

From that we write an individual plan for a 3-month cycle, matched to the school's trimesters, with an assessment month in December. Your child attends school sessions 4 days a week for 2 hours, one 2-hour group session a week led by a psychologist for turn-taking and peer play, and 2 to 4 one-to-one therapy sessions a week depending on the plan. Those might be occupational therapy for fine motor skills and sensory regulation, speech and language therapy, special education at your child's own pace, or a combination.

You get the plan in writing, and then a weekly review, so you are not waiting until December to find out how it is going. Therapists hand strategies to the teachers daily and the teachers report back what actually happened in class, which is how a plan stays honest. Alongside it you get take-home worksheets matched to your child's level, help building a routine at home, and monthly coaching.

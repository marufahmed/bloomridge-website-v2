There is a particular silence that falls when your child is screaming on the floor of a shop and 6 adults have stopped to watch. Someone will offer advice. Someone else will say something about discipline, loudly enough for you to hear.

Most parents are handed one explanation for all of it: the child is spoiled. That explanation is wrong often enough to be worth questioning, because two very different things look almost identical from the outside.

Knowing which one you are looking at changes what you do. And what you do changes how long it lasts, and what your child learns from it.

## What a tantrum is

A tantrum has a goal. Your child wants the packet of chips, wants the tablet back, wants to stay at the park. The behaviour is loud and unpleasant, but it is pointed at something.

You can usually spot it by where your child is looking. They check your face. They pause to see whether the crying is working, then restart at a higher volume if it is not. They tend to stay just inside safety, choosing somewhere soft to throw themselves, and they stop reasonably quickly once the goal is reached or once it is completely and calmly off the table.

This is not wickedness. It is a child testing whether a rule holds, which is a normal thing for a 3-year-old to do. What helps is being calm, boring and consistent. Name the want out loud so your child feels heard, hold the line without a speech, and offer what is actually available. A no that turns into a yes after 10 minutes of screaming teaches that 10 minutes of screaming is the price.

## What a meltdown is

A meltdown is overload. The nervous system has taken in more than it can sort, and it has stopped coping. There is no goal and there is no plan, and your child is not choosing it.

This is why reasoning, bribes and threats all bounce off. Understanding language is one of the first things to go, so a calm explanation is just more noise arriving at a brain that is already full. Adding your raised voice, or 3 concerned relatives leaning in, makes it worse. A meltdown needs less input, not more.

It often ends in exhaustion rather than satisfaction: sobbing that trails off, sleep, or a long need to be held. Afterwards your child may not be able to tell you what happened, because they genuinely do not know.

Meltdowns are far more likely when the day has already been full. A hot rickshaw ride in traffic, a wedding the night before with music until 1am, no proper lunch, then one small request at 6pm. The last thing is rarely the real cause.

The honest complication is that the same child has both, and a tantrum that goes on long enough can tip into a meltdown. A useful test afterwards is simple: did it stop the moment they got what they wanted, and were they watching you throughout?

## What to do in the moment

Fewer words. Speak lower and slower than feels natural, and say the same short phrase, something like "I am here". Reduce what is coming in: step out of the aisle, move to a stairwell or a quieter corner, turn the lights down at home, ask well-meaning relatives to give some space.

Stay close, but do not demand eye contact, answers or a hug. Some children want firm pressure, a hand on the back or being held tightly, and some cannot bear to be touched at that moment. Your child's Student Profile should record which, so that every adult does the same thing.

Do not teach during a meltdown. Nothing you say will be stored. Keep your child safe from injury, keep yourself calm enough to be a steady presence, and wait it out.

About the watching strangers: you do not owe anyone an explanation while your child is in distress. "He is having a hard time, we are alright" said once is plenty. The shame you feel is real, but it belongs to the situation, not to you, and no parent who is standing calmly beside their child in public is doing it wrong.

## Afterwards, and spotting the pattern

Once your child is settled, think about water, food and quiet before anything else. Repair matters more than analysis, so a short and warm "that was hard, we got through it" beats a series of questions your child cannot answer.

If a rule was genuinely broken, come back to it later in 1 sentence, when everyone is calm. Never ask a child to apologise to a room full of adults.

Then look for the shape of it. Write down 2 weeks of hard moments with 4 details: the time, what your child had eaten, what was being asked, and what the noise and light were like. Most families find 2 or 3 reliable triggers within a fortnight. Very often it is late morning or the stretch between 5pm and 7pm, hunger, and transitions: leaving the park, turning off the television, getting into a crowded housing-society lift.

Once you know the pattern you can change the setup rather than the child. Snack first. Warning first. Hard errands early in the day.

Every plan here includes one 2-hour psychologist-led group session a week, where children practise turn-taking, peer play and emotional awareness. A large part of that work is helping a child notice their own state early, so that "my body feels fast" arrives before the point of no return, and so that waiting is practised when it is only a little bit hard, with an adult nearby.

The Student Profile keeps everyone consistent. It records sensory needs, communication preferences and motivators, so the teacher in the school session, the therapist in the one-to-one session and you at home all use the same signal and the same short phrase. Consistency is what makes meltdowns less frequent over a term, and progress is tracked on a 4-step scale, emerging, developing, consistent, independent, rather than by how the week felt.

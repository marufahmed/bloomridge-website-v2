It usually starts with advice from someone who means well. Your child is 2 and a half and still not talking much, and an aunt says the problem is obvious: too many languages. Bangla with the grandparents, English at school, cartoons in English, a bit of Hindi from somewhere. Pick one, she says, and the words will come.

It is one of the most common things we hear from parents in Banasree, and it is not true. Growing up with 2 languages does not cause speech delay. Children across the world have grown up bilingual for as long as there have been children, and the research on this is unusually consistent.

That does not mean your worry is misplaced. If your child is genuinely behind, he is behind whether he hears one language or three, and it needs looking at. What is worth getting right is what you would actually be fixing, because dropping Bangla fixes nothing and costs a great deal.

## What bilingualism does and does not do

A bilingual child is learning the same amount of language as anyone else, just distributed across two systems. The total load is comparable. What changes is where the words sit.

Two things can look like a delay and are not. First, a bilingual child may know fewer words in each language separately than a child who hears only one. That is arithmetic, not difficulty, and the gap usually closes over the school years. Second, one language is often stronger, and which one can swap over time. A child who was all Bangla at 3 may be mostly English by 6 if school runs in English all day.

Some children also go through a quiet period when a new language arrives suddenly, for instance in the first months at an English-medium school. They listen far more than they speak. That is normal and temporary, and not the same as a delay that was there beforehand.

## Count both languages together

This is the single most useful thing in this article. When someone asks how many words your child has, the answer is the total across both languages, not the English list.

If your child says "pani", "dudh", "ball", "up", "Nanu" and "more", that is 6 words, even though an English-only count gives 3. Words that exist in both languages count once. Your child's vocabulary is the number of things he can name and ask for, not the number he can name in the language a particular adult happens to prefer.

The same applies to putting words together. "Aro juice" is a 2-word combination. So is "Baba jabe". Mixing the languages inside one sentence does not make it count for less.

This is part of the reason we ask you to bring a note of the words your child uses, in whatever language they come out in. You hear your child all day. We will hear him for an hour or two, in an unfamiliar room, and he may be too busy watching us to use half of what he has.

## Mixing the two is normal

Parents often worry most about sentences like "Ami want juice" or "give me pani". It sounds like confusion. It is not.

Switching languages inside a sentence is something fluent bilingual adults do constantly, including most of Dhaka. A child does it for a simple reason: he reaches for the word he knows best, and that word happens to sit in the other language. He is not mixing the two up. He is using everything he has.

Children work out who speaks which language remarkably early. A 3-year-old who chats in English with a teacher and switches to Bangla the moment his grandmother walks in is showing a sophisticated skill, not a problem.

## Do not drop the home language

If you take one thing away, take this. When families are told to drop Bangla and speak only English at home, several things tend to go wrong at once.

Parents end up speaking to their child in their weaker language, with fewer words, fewer songs and less of the easy warmth that makes a child want to talk at all. The grandmother who spends the most hours with the child is suddenly cut out, or asked to use English she does not have. And the child loses the language of his own family, which matters for reasons that have nothing to do with therapy.

A rich home language is not a rival to English. It is the foundation English gets built on. Speak whichever language you are most fluent and most yourself in, and let school do the work it is there for.

## If there really is a delay

Bilingualism does not cause delay, but it does not protect against it either. Some bilingual children do have a genuine speech and language difficulty, and the way to tell is to look at both languages together.

The signal is a delay across both. A 2-year-old with fewer than 50 words in Bangla and English combined, or no 2-word combinations by around 2 and a half, is worth checking however many languages he hears. So is a 3-year-old the family struggles to understand in either language. A child who is fluent in Bangla and quiet in English does not have a language delay. He has a second language that is still arriving.

It is also worth having your child's hearing checked by your paediatrician first, because repeated ear infections muffle both languages equally and are easy to miss.

Parents usually nod politely when we say occupational therapy, and then ask on the way out what sort of job we are preparing a 4-year-old for. It is a fair question. The name is old and it does not describe what happens in the room.

Here is the short version. Occupational therapy is about the things that occupy your child's day: holding a spoon, doing up a shirt button, sitting through a 20-minute lesson, tolerating a crowded room, using the toilet without help, getting a pencil to go where the eyes want it to go.

If your child is bright and chatty but cannot manage buttons, or can build anything out of blocks but will not sit at a table for 5 minutes, that is not a contradiction. These are separate skills, and they can be taught.

## Why it is called occupational therapy

In this field, occupation means whatever occupies you. For an adult that may be work. For a 3-year-old it is play, dressing, eating and learning to be part of a group. An occupational therapist looks at what a child needs to do each day, works out which part is breaking down, and builds that part up.

The work sits in 3 areas that overlap constantly: fine motor skills, sensory regulation and daily independence. A child rarely struggles neatly in only one.

## Hands that can do what the day asks

Fine motor skill is what the small muscles of the hand can do. It develops in a fairly reliable order. First a whole-fist grasp, then a thumb working against the fingers, then the small precise movements that let a child pick up a single bead or turn a page without tearing it.

When that development is slow, you see it in ordinary places. Rice slides off the spoon, so your child eats with a fist or gives up and asks to be fed. School shirt buttons defeat them, and a grandmother does the buttons every morning because it is faster. Scissors are held sideways and the paper folds instead of cutting.

Handwriting is where this catches up with a child at school, and by then it is not really a hand problem. A child needs a steady trunk and shoulder before the fingers can do fine work, in the same way you need a steady tripod before a camera is any use. So we often start further up the body: climbing, hanging, pushing, drawing big shapes on a wall-mounted sheet, and only later worry about letter shapes.

## A body settled enough to learn

Sensory regulation is the second strand. Some children take in too much sound, light or touch, and spend the day flinching. Others take in too little and go hunting for it by crashing, chewing or spinning. Either way, a child who is fighting their own nervous system has nothing left over for learning.

An occupational therapist works out what settles a particular child, then builds it into the day before it is needed. Deep pressure is the usual workhorse: a firm squeeze through the arms, pushing a heavy basket, a weighted lap cushion for a short while. Swinging helps some children organise themselves, while for others the same swing is far too much. Textured play with rice, dough, foam or water gives a child a safe place to practise tolerating touch they would usually refuse.

None of this is a trick to make a child sit still. It is about getting a child into the state where sitting still is possible, which is a different thing and lasts longer.

## Getting dressed, eating and sitting at a desk

Daily independence is the part parents feel most. Pulling trousers up without help. Managing the toilet at school without waiting for an adult. Eating a meal at the same pace as everyone else. Putting on shoes before the rickshaw arrives, rather than 10 minutes after.

These skills matter for more than convenience. A 5-year-old who can dress themselves is not the child who is singled out every day, and the confidence from that shows up in how they speak to other children. Sitting tolerance works the same way. A child who can stay in a chair for 15 minutes gets access to everything that happens at the table, and a child who lasts 3 minutes does not.

We break each of these down into steps and teach the last step first, so your child gets the feeling of having finished something. Pulling the zip the last 5 centimetres is where we begin, then a little more each week.

## What a session looks like, and how it reaches the classroom

From the door it looks like play, and for the child it should. Threading beads onto a lace is pincer grip practice. Hiding small beads inside stiff playdough and digging them out is hand strength. An obstacle course of climbing, crawling and pushing is trunk and shoulder work and often a way of settling a child before table work. Tongs, tearing paper, chalk on a vertical board, a sensory swing for 5 minutes before anything is asked of them: all of it has a target behind it.

What makes the difference is what happens after the session. Every plan includes school sessions 4 days a week for 2 hours, one 2-hour psychologist-led group session a week, and 2 to 4 one-to-one therapy sessions a week depending on whether the plan is Foundation, Enhanced or Intensive. Each day the therapist hands the teacher the strategies that are working, and the teacher feeds back what actually happened in class. So "he needs 5 minutes on the swing before table work" becomes a routine, and "he sat for 12 minutes today, up from 4 last month" comes back to the therapist and to you.

All of it runs from a live Student Profile recording learning style, communication preferences, sensory needs and motivators, so your child does not have to prove themselves again to every new adult. Goals are written for 3-month cycles and scored on a 4-step scale: emerging, developing, consistent, independent. You get a written review each week, worksheets matched to your child's level, and monthly coaching.

We do not diagnose. If your child's difficulties may have a medical cause, a paediatrician or developmental specialist is the right person to see, and we will say so.

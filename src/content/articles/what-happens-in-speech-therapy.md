Parents often arrive for a first speech session braced for something clinical. They picture a desk, a chart, a child asked to repeat words and getting them wrong while everyone watches.

It is almost nothing like that. A first speech and language therapy session looks, to anyone glancing in, like a grown-up playing on the floor with a child. That is deliberate. Children show you what they can really do when they are enjoying themselves, and very little when they are being tested.

Knowing the shape of the session in advance helps. It helps your child too, because a parent who is relaxed in the doorway is the biggest thing standing between a 3-year-old and a good first 10 minutes.

## The first 10 minutes

The therapist will usually start on the floor, not at the table. Shoes off, a small box of things chosen before you arrived: bubbles, a ball, a set of stacking cups, a few picture cards, a toy car, sometimes a mirror.

Nothing is handed over at once. The therapist puts out 2 or 3 things and waits, watching which one your child goes towards. That choice is the first piece of information. A child who goes straight for the bubbles is telling us something different from a child who lines the cups up along the edge of the mat.

Then the therapist follows your child's lead. If the car is what matters, the session is about the car. The words come to the child rather than the child being marched towards the words: "car", "go", "car go", "fast car", said clearly and repeated, at a level just slightly above what your child is producing on his own. This is called modelling, and it is most of the work.

Bubbles earn their place because they create a natural pause. Blow once, then stop and hold the pot. Your child now has a reason to communicate, and whatever comes out, a look, a reach, a sound, a word, is real rather than a performance for an adult. The mirror is for children working on speech sounds, so they can see where their lips and tongue go.

Some of the session moves to a small table, especially with older children also working on sitting tolerance for school. Picture cards come out there. But the table is part of the session, not the whole of it.

## Receptive and expressive language

You will hear these two words, so here is what they mean without the jargon.

Receptive language is what your child understands. Can he follow "put the cup on the chair" without you pointing. Does he know what a spoon is when you name it among 4 objects. Does he understand "in", "under", "not now".

Expressive language is what your child can get out. Words, signs, gestures, pointing, sentences, asking for help. All of it counts.

These two can be far apart, and knowing which is which changes the plan. A child who understands a great deal and says little needs different support from a child talking in long strings that do not quite connect to what is happening around him. Understanding almost always runs ahead of speaking, which is why so many toddlers follow instructions long before they can argue with them.

## What the therapist is watching for

Behind the play, the therapist is collecting a specific set of observations. How your child asks for something he wants, and whether he asks at all or simply takes. Whether he looks at your face to check you have understood. How long he can stay with one activity before he needs to move.

Also: which sounds he can make and which he leaves off, whether the same word comes out the same way twice, how he copes when something ends before he wanted it to. What he does when he is frustrated, because that tells us how much of the difficulty is language and how much is regulation.

And your child's motivators, which sounds small and is not. A child who will do almost anything for a turn with the bubble pot is a child we can teach. Everything we build later hangs on knowing what he cares about.

## Where the goals go

None of this stays in the therapist's head. Everything observed goes into your child's Student Profile, a living document that records learning style, communication preferences, sensory needs and motivators. Every therapist, teacher and caregiver working with your child works from the same profile, so nobody is starting from scratch on a Tuesday morning.

Goals are written in small, specific steps rather than broad hopes. Not "improve communication" but "requests a desired object using a single word in 3 out of 5 opportunities". Each skill sits somewhere on a 4-step scale: emerging, developing, consistent, independent. Emerging means it has been seen once or twice with heavy support. Independent means your child does it without prompting, in more than one place, with more than one person.

The loop runs daily. The speech therapist hands practical strategies to the classroom teacher, the teacher tries them in a group of children and reports back what happened, and the therapist adjusts. A weekly review is shared with you, so you are not waiting 3 months to find out how it is going. Plans themselves are written for 3-month cycles, matched to the school trimesters, with an assessment month in December.

## What you take home

At the end of the session the therapist will show you 2 or 3 things to do at home. Not 10. Two or three, chosen because they fit your day.

They tend to be small changes to things you already do. Pause before handing over the water glass and wait 5 seconds. Put the biscuit packet where it can be seen but not reached, so there is a reason to ask. Name what your child is looking at instead of asking questions about it, because a running commentary teaches more words than a quiz.

You will also get worksheets and activity guides matched to your child's level, and help shaping a home routine that survives real life, including the evenings when load-shedding takes out the fan and nobody is at their best. Monthly coaching keeps it going. The centre sessions matter, but the hours between them are where most learning sticks.

Most parents notice it quietly. Your child is 2, a cousin born a month later is already stringing words together, and yours pulls you by the hand to the fridge and waits for you to guess. You tell yourself he will catch up. Then someone at a family gathering asks why he is not talking yet, and the worry moves to the front of your mind.

That worry deserves a straight answer rather than a pat on the shoulder. Some children talk late and then catch up on their own. Others need help, and the earlier it starts, the less ground there is to make up. The only way to tell which is which is to look closely at what your child is doing now, in every language spoken at home, and compare it with what is usual for the age.

What follows is that comparison. It is not a test and it is not a diagnosis. Children vary, and a child a few weeks behind one marker is not a child in trouble. What matters is the whole picture, and whether it keeps moving forward.

## What is usual, age by age

At 12 months, most children babble in long runs of different sounds, with a rise and fall that sounds like conversation without the words. They use their bodies to communicate: pointing at what they want, lifting their arms to be picked up, waving at the neighbour in the lift, pushing a bowl away. A first word or two may have arrived. At this age, gestures count as much as sounds.

At 18 months, most children use roughly 10 to 20 words of their own. They do not have to be clear. "Ba" for ball and "amma" both count, as long as your child says them on purpose and means the same thing each time. They also understand far more than they can say. Fetch your shoes, give it to Nanu.

At 2 years, most children have somewhere near 50 words and have begun joining 2 of them: "more juice", "Baba go", "no bath". The 2-word combination matters more than the exact count. It shows your child has worked out that words can be put together to make new meaning, and that is the doorway to sentences.

At 3 years, most children talk in short sentences of 3 or 4 words, and someone outside the family can follow about 75 per cent of what they say. Not all of it. A new teacher or a visiting aunt should mostly manage without you translating. Sounds like r, s, l and th are often still settling at 3, and that is normal.

## Late talker, or something wider

There is a distinction here, and it is the one a therapist looks for first. A late talker is a child whose understanding, social interest and play are on track, but whose spoken words are slow to arrive. A broader communication concern shows up in more than words.

Watch for three things. Does your child point at things to show you, not only to get them, the way a toddler points at a crow on the balcony rail and looks back at your face. Does your child share attention, glancing between the toy and you to check you have noticed. Does your child turn when you call his name across the room.

If those three are steady and only the words are missing, you are most likely looking at a late talker. If two or three of them are shaky, or if your child once had words and then stopped using them, that is worth looking into sooner rather than later. Loss of words a child previously used is always a reason to seek advice.

Bloomridge Springs does not diagnose, and no article can. If you are seeing that wider pattern, speak to your paediatrician, who can refer you to a developmental specialist. Support and assessment can run alongside that.

## Hearing comes first

Before anything else, get your child's hearing checked. This gets skipped surprisingly often, because a child who responds to the doorbell and to loud cartoons seems obviously fine.

Repeated ear infections, or fluid sitting behind the eardrum, can muffle speech sounds for months without causing pain. A child hearing everything through a blanket will be slow to copy words, and there is nothing wrong with the child at all. Your paediatrician or an ENT can arrange a hearing test.

## Two languages at home is not the reason

In Banasree this comes up in nearly every conversation. Bangla with the grandparents, English at the school gate, cartoons in English. Parents worry they have confused their child, and some well-meaning relative suggests dropping a language.

Growing up with 2 languages does not cause speech delay. A bilingual child's words are spread across both, so counting only the English ones makes the total look far smaller than it is. Count them together. We have a separate article on this.

## Waiting and seeing is not the only option

"Give him time" is sometimes right. The trouble is that it is offered as the only choice. There is a middle path between anxious waiting and assuming the worst: getting a clear picture of where your child is now.

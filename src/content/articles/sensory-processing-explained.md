Some children cover their ears before anyone else has noticed the noise. Some cannot bear the seam inside a sock, or scream through a haircut they sat through happily 3 months ago. Others crash into the sofa all evening, chew their collar, or spin until they fall over.

If this is your child, someone has probably told you they are being stubborn, or that you are too soft, or that they will grow out of it. You may have carried a screaming 4-year-old out of a wedding hall while 40 relatives watched. That stays with a parent.

There is usually something else going on, and it has a name. Sensory processing is how your child's brain takes in information from the body and the world, sorts it, and decides what to do with it. When that sorting works differently, ordinary places become hard work.

## What sensory processing means

Think of the brain as a sorting office. Every second, messages arrive: the fan overhead, the label at the back of the neck, the pressure of feet on the floor. Most brains quietly file away what does not matter, so a child can pay attention to the thing that does, which might be your voice or a picture book.

For some children the sorting office is swamped. Everything arrives at the same volume, so the ceiling fan is as loud as you are. For others the messages arrive faint, so the child goes hunting for more.

Most of us learned about 5 senses at school. There are 8 that matter here. Alongside sight, sound, touch, taste and smell are 3 that rarely get named. Vestibular is balance and movement, run by the inner ear, telling your child where their head is and whether they are tipping. Proprioception is body position, coming from muscles and joints, telling a child where their arms and legs are without looking. Interoception is the internal sense: hunger, thirst, tiredness, a full bladder.

Those last 3 explain a great deal. A child who cannot easily feel where their body is will lean on people, press against walls and grip a pencil hard enough to tear the page. A child who does not read internal signals may not notice they need the toilet in time.

## Over-responsive, under-responsive and seeking

Three patterns show up again and again. Over-responsive means the volume is turned up: sound, light, touch or smell lands harder than it would for you, so a child flinches, covers up or runs. Under-responsive means the volume is turned down: no reaction to a call across the room, no fuss about a scraped knee. Seeking means the child goes looking for input: crashing, jumping, chewing, spinning.

Most children are a mixture, and it shifts with tiredness and hunger.

You will recognise the settings. A wedding is 100 people, loud music, sharp lights and a child passed from one relative to the next. A barber's chair in Banasree is clippers buzzing near the ears, hair on a bare neck and a tipped-back seat that takes the ground away. Mealtimes go wrong over texture more often than taste, so plain rice is fine and anything wet or mixed is refused. A crowded housing-society lift is a metal box, a press of bodies and a drop in the stomach, and your child either screams or goes limp.

## Why regulation comes before learning

Regulation is keeping the body and the feelings in a workable range: alert but calm, not wound up and not shut down. It is not the same as being quiet or obedient. A regulated child can hear an instruction, wait 30 seconds and try something difficult.

A child who is fighting their own nervous system cannot also learn letters. This is why our first job in a session is often not the task at all. It is getting a child to a state where the task is possible, and only then putting the work in front of them.

It is also why we write down what settles a particular child. Sensory needs sit in a live Student Profile with learning style, communication preferences and motivators, so teacher, therapist and caregiver all use the same things.

## 5 things to try at home

1. Heavy work before the hard part. Carrying water bottles, pushing a loaded laundry basket or 10 big jumps gives deep pressure through muscles and joints, which settles most children. Do it 10 minutes before the haircut, not during.
2. Say what is coming, in few words. "2 more turns on the slide, then shoes." Look at the barber's chair the day before, and go at 11am when the shop is empty rather than on a Friday evening.
3. Change the clothes, not the child. Cut labels out, wash new clothes twice before wearing, try seamless socks. A scratchy seam is a real signal.
4. Make one quiet corner. A mattress behind a door, a bedsheet over a clothes rack, 2 or 3 toys. It must be somewhere your child chooses to go, never a punishment. During load-shedding, when the fans stop and the building fills with generator noise, that corner earns its keep.
5. Keep notes for 1 week. Time, place, what happened in the 10 minutes before. Patterns turn up fast: almost always 5pm, or every time the lift is full.

## How occupational therapy helps

Occupational therapy is not about jobs. For a young child, the occupations are playing, dressing, eating, writing and getting through a school day. An occupational therapist works on fine motor skills such as grip, buttons and spoons, on sensory regulation and on daily independence.

We begin with a strengths-first assessment, mapping what your child can already do before we look at what is hard. Plans include school sessions 4 days a week for 2 hours, one 2-hour psychologist-led group session a week, and 2 to 4 one-to-one therapy sessions. Therapists hand strategies to teachers daily, teachers report back what happened in class, and you get a written review each week. Skills are scored on a 4-step scale, emerging, developing, consistent, independent, so "he is better with noise" becomes something you can see on paper.

We do not diagnose, and children come to us with and without one. If there might be a medical cause, for example pain during haircuts or a hearing difficulty, a paediatrician or developmental specialist should see your child, and we will say so.
